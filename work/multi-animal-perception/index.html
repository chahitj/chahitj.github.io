Crowded-pen monitoring

Commercial swine monitoring needs per-animal observations in crowded pens, but a camera often sees many similar-looking pigs touching or overlapping. Counting animals, locating body parts, and keeping identity across time are different problems; good detections alone do not solve tracking.

Available annotated data varied in camera angle, illumination, pose convention, and quality. We needed a consistent anatomical keypoint schema before model scores would mean much. The work focused on dataset curation and model evaluation for a future monitoring system.

Data in practice

Repeated views and overlap make identity harder than single-frame detection.

Detection, pose, and identity

Camera frames feed YOLO detection, pose estimation, and DeepSORT association. ViTPose, HRNet, and SLEAP supported the pose investigations. Maintaining one animal's identity after complete occlusion remained unresolved.

Perception pipeline

Dataset curation and annotation

From fragmented data to a custom annotation schema

The project reviewed 38 videos and 2,146 frames representing 374 pigs and roughly 22,000 annotated instances, with annotations sampled every 15 frames. After simpler public-data experiments, the team developed a final 22-keypoint anatomical schema with veterinary input. I annotated about 100 images containing more than 1,000 pig instances. Our six-person team then reassigned dataset portions for cross-review: pig class, occlusion, and anatomical points needed to mean the same thing across annotators. The intermediate 12-keypoint benchmark is separate from this final dataset.

Choosing a perception stack

The work covered YOLO detection, DeepSORT tracking, and ViTPose, HRNet, and SLEAP pose experiments. I used an OpenCV region-of-interest/background mask to keep irrelevant pen areas out of the detector and capped candidate detections at a physically plausible pig count.

Lighting and domain shift

We found a severe day/night performance gap. I led the augmentation work, starting with controlled photometric transformations and extending it with GAN-based day-to-night generation. We added the augmented nighttime data back into training and retrained the models.

Dense tracking and identity

Detection and short-term association were exercised in dense scenes. When an animal disappears fully behind others and returns, visual similarity makes its old identity hard to recover. That full-occlusion case remained unsolved.

Edge inference investigation

I wrote inference scripts for NVIDIA Jetson and Raspberry Pi 4/5 hardware and debugged video input and model execution, establishing the path from trained models to edge-device testing.

Perturbation robustness

Lighting and perturbation tests

An intermediate 12-keypoint YOLOv8-m-pose run measured box mAP@50 of 0.992 and keypoint mAP@50 of 0.986. The later 22-keypoint work focused on consistent anatomy, lighting robustness, and dense occlusion; the retained records contain qualitative outputs rather than a final comparable benchmark.

Synthetic darkening exposed the lighting sensitivity. After we added photometric and GAN-generated nighttime data to training, held-out nighttime behavior improved. The surviving records do not support an exact post-intervention AP figure.

Day-to-night lighting transformation

The paired views hold the scene constant and apply a synthetic darkening transform, making the lighting shift easy to inspect before retraining with augmented data.

Failure analysis

Lighting and occlusion

Overlap can join parts of neighboring animals into plausible but incorrect poses. The harder failure was identity: after a pig vanished completely behind others, short-term association could not reliably determine which similar animal reappeared. This remains distinct from detection or pose accuracy on visible frames.
