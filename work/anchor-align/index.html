Research problem

A VLA can execute a familiar manipulation trajectory while failing to ground the instruction in the current image. Rearranging objects, adding distractors, or changing the named target exposes this gap between motor execution and visual-language understanding.

Anchor-Align studies whether a frozen reference VLM can preserve useful visual-language representations as the policy learns actions. I contributed feedback during method development and worked on the physical evaluation: collecting demonstrations, deploying two VLA implementations, and making their behavior under changed scenes interpretable.

Method

The team's method aligns a finetuned policy's representations with a frozen reference VLM and couples language and action supervision. We evaluated it with VLA-Adapter and StarVLA, two distinct VLA implementations, to test whether the effect carried beyond one policy architecture.

  • VLA-Adapter (Yihao Wang et al., 2025)

    The real-world configuration used a bridge-attention action head trained with an L1 regression objective.

    VLA-Adapter: An Effective Paradigm for Tiny-Scale Vision-Language-Action Model
  • StarVLA (StarVLA Community, 2026)

    The real-world configuration paired Qwen2.5-VL with a GR00T FM-DiT flow-matching action head, testing the method across a different policy architecture.

    StarVLA: A Lego-like Codebase for Vision-Language-Action Model Developing

Method overview

Anchor-Align preserves reference visual-language representations while the policy learns actions. The xArm7 study measures how that intervention changes behavior under controlled scene shifts.

Spatial and semantic distribution shifts

Spatial rearrangement

Move the target and surrounding objects away from their training positions. Tests whether the policy responds to the current layout instead of repeating a familiar motion.

Clutter

Add distractors and ask for a specific object among them. The surrounding objects make target selection visible before the final placement.

Compositional object-layout

Change object placement and orientation together. Stresses both spatial reasoning and object recognition simultaneously.

Semantic target

Ask for the pink mug after training on green. Tested with StarVLA only. Separates language grounding from spatial generalization.

Physical evaluation

I collected demonstrations with a GELLO teleoperation device, deployed VLA-Adapter and StarVLA on the xArm7, and matched RealSense camera serials and views to the training configuration. When a camera moved or an observation was mismatched, I recalibrated and checked the full image path before treating the rollout as a model failure.

The xArm7 deployment work also included exploratory π0.5 testing. The reported Anchor-Align comparisons below use VLA-Adapter and StarVLA.

I tuned how many predicted action steps the arm executed per inference call. Longer chunks could produce abrupt motion; shorter chunks increased inference frequency and could make the arm hesitate. I added overlapping inference so the next prediction could begin before the previous chunk ended, then blended adjacent chunks to reduce discontinuities during physical execution.

The real-world study used 20 rollouts per condition. Three shifts were evaluated with both VLA-Adapter and StarVLA; the pink-mug semantic shift was tested with StarVLA only. I ran and reviewed physical rollouts, then labeled behavior with a six-mode failure taxonomy rather than relying on success alone.

Across the three shared real-robot conditions, the team study reports mean success increasing from 28.3% to 54.2% for VLA-Adapter and from 36.7% to 60.0% for StarVLA.

Failure analysis

Real-world failure modes

The six-mode taxonomy distinguishes Incorrect Object, Semantic Error, Memorization Error, Grasp Failure, Grasp-and-Drop Error, and Close Miss. It separates failures in target selection from failures after the robot has reached the right object.

Correct target selection did not guarantee completion. Grasp, drop, and close-miss failures remained even when the robot approached the right object. I had to separate those policy failures from camera configuration, calibration, and action-chunk behavior; otherwise the hardware could make the research result appear better or worse for the wrong reason.

Six failure modes

  1. Incorrect object

    The policy approaches the wrong object. Target selection fails before grasp quality can matter.

  2. Semantic error

    A feature-similar distractor is selected instead of the instructed target.

  3. Memorization error

    The robot follows a familiar training trajectory despite a changed scene.

  4. Grasp failure

    The robot reaches the target but does not descend to complete the grasp.

  5. Grasp-and-drop error

    The target is grasped, but release is premature, too high, or missing.

  6. Close miss

    The policy grounds the target correctly but misses by a small margin.
